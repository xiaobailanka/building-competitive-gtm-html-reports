# Competitive-analysis SOP

This skill operationalizes the following sequence. The order matters because each stage constrains the next one.

## 1. Define the analysis goal

State why the analysis exists before collecting facts. Typical goals:

- new-product launch / GTM;
- selling-point positioning;
- pricing and SKU design;
- media and creator strategy;
- e-commerce conversion optimization;
- market-opportunity diagnosis.

Do not mix every possible goal. Pick one primary decision and up to two supporting decisions.

## 2. Select competitors correctly

### Direct competitor

Similar product form, same core demand, overlapping target user, comparable price band, and realistic purchase substitution.

Analyze deeply: price, SKU ladder, features, claims, channels, funnel, launch actions, reviews, conversion logic.

### Indirect competitor

Solves the same user need through a different product form, ecosystem, or value proposition.

Use to understand alternatives and substitution, not as the core spec table.

### Reference competitor

Not a true substitute, but worth learning from for content format, creator mix, launch mechanics, merchandising, or conversion design.

Never present a reference competitor as a direct competitor.

## 3. Decompose user and positioning

Do not use shallow labels such as “young people”, “students”, or “high-end users”. Map:

- use cases;
- buying motivation;
- ability/willingness to pay;
- decision pain points;
- who uses / who pays / who influences;
- search questions;
- switching trigger;
- reason to buy now.

For each competitor, answer: **What choice is this brand trying to become in the consumer's mind?**

When demographic survey evidence is unavailable, describe **need-state segments** and label them analytical inference.

## 4. Decompose product and selling points

Inspect at least:

- core function;
- headline selling points;
- price/SKU architecture;
- usage threshold / learning cost;
- meaningful differentiation;
- trust endorsement;
- “why buy now?” trigger;
- ownership cost / service / warranty where relevant.

Official pages reveal what the brand wants to say. Reviews, comparisons, community feedback, channel pages, and first-party user data reveal what users actually notice.

## 5. Decompose channels and content

Analyze platforms by **role in the journey**, not by screenshot collection.

For each relevant channel ask:

- discovery, search, trust, comparison, or conversion role?
- dominant content format?
- recurring search terms / user questions?
- creator archetypes?
- repeatable content matrix or only one viral hit?
- commerce handoff?
- media amplification?

If platform search volume, view counts, or conversion data cannot be reliably verified, do not invent them. Convert the gap into a proposed first-party measurement plan.

## 6. Decompose the conversion journey

Canonical path:

`Exposure → Interest/Seeding → Search → Comparison → Purchase → Retention/Referral`

For complex categories add trial/demo, financing, store visit, quotation, onboarding, etc.

At each stage identify:

- consumer question;
- competitor advantage;
- our friction;
- evidence needed;
- operating action;
- measurement signal.

## 7. Output conclusions, not inventory

The final analysis must explicitly answer:

1. What did the competitor do right?
2. What is weak, overclaimed, or vulnerable?
3. Where is our differentiated opportunity?
4. What should we test next?
5. What should we **not** copy?

A report without a judgment and next action is incomplete.

## 8. Common mistakes to prevent

- listing information without judgment;
- reading only official pages and ignoring user evidence;
- making a copycat plan instead of learning the mechanism;
- over-indexing on one viral post;
- comparing non-equivalent SKUs or time windows;
- turning rumor into fact;
- mixing market-share definitions from different research firms;
- calling clicks or views “conversion”;
- ignoring price, financing, stock, service and fulfillment;
- using a beautiful chart for numbers that are not comparable.
